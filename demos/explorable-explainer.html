How does a mechanical keyboard switch work?

A plastic stem, a steel spring and two strips of gold-plated metal. Halfway down, the computer already knows. The other half of the press is up to you.

You have probably pressed a key a few million times without wondering what happens under it. On a mechanical keyboard every key sits on its own small machine: a switch square that turns a push into a closed circuit, and then pushes your finger back.

This page takes one apart. The numbers come from Cherry's datasheet for its MX Red, the classic "linear" switch, and every readout below is computed from them.

What is inside one key?

Six parts stack along a single axis. The keycap is the part you touch. It grips the cross-shaped top of the stem, the only part that travels. The stem rides inside the top housing, which keeps it straight, and presses on a steel spring. Down in the bottom housing, two thin contact leaves wait for the stem to let them touch, and metal pins carry their signal into the circuit board.

Below, the switch comes apart and goes back together. Drag the slider to hold it open, and point at a layer, or tap its name, to pick it out.

Nothing in this stack is electronic except those two leaves. Everything else is there to move them at the right moment, and to make the motion feel the same on the hundred-millionth press as on the first.

When does the key count as pressed?

Press the key slowly and three things change together. The stem slides down, the spring shortens and pushes back harder, and at one exact depth the contact closes.

The spring already pushes up with before you touch the key, about the weight of six sheets of printer paper. The resistance then climbs in a straight line, and at it reaches . That depth is the actuation point. There the stem's side leg slides past the bent tip of the moving leaf, the leaf springs across onto the fixed one, and the circuit closes.

The key is only halfway down. The stem can travel in all, and the last two millimetres do nothing for the computer. At the bottom the stem lands on the housing and the line turns into a wall. Cherry lists as the final force, but by then you are pressing on plastic, not on the spring.

Why go all the way down?

Most people do, at least some of the time. The extra travel costs nothing in time: the computer hears the press the moment the stem passes , and whatever your finger does after that cannot make the signal arrive sooner.

Below, two presses start at the same instant and at the same speed. Press A eases off just after the actuation point and turns around at . Press B goes all the way to the floor. One clock along the bottom drives both.

Both keys register at the same moment. For a few milliseconds after that the leaves bounce against each other, and the keyboard waits it out before it believes the press. Press B then does about twice the work, against , and spends the rest of its energy hitting the floor.

That is not much per press. Over letters a day the difference comes to about , roughly the work of lifting a one-kilogram bag of sugar a metre. It arrives as a small knock at the end of every stroke, which is why some typists teach themselves to stop short.

Which switch works hardest?

Every key on the board has the same switch, but they do not get the same life. In English text, e makes up about 12.7% of letters and z about 0.07%, so the switch under E takes about times as many presses as the one under Z.

Below, those shares are written on the keyboard itself, flat, like a table. Then the plan lifts into the room: each key rises in proportion to its share, and every key stays exactly where it was.

Even the E switch is in no danger. At letters a day it sees about presses, and Cherry rates the switch for more than 100 million, which works out to roughly . The keycap will be shiny long before the switch under it notices.

So the next time you type, notice where your fingers stop. The computer stopped listening at two millimetres.
