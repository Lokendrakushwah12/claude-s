How does a camera shutter work?

Two curtains of thin metal blades, each thrown by a spring. The exposure is not how fast they move but how long the second one waits, and at fast speeds the picture is never uncovered all at once.

You have probably heard it thousands of times: the small double click when a camera takes a picture. Inside that click the camera lets light onto the sensor for an exact sliver of time, often a thousandth of a second, and then shuts it out completely.

A thousandth of a second is short. A sprinter at full speed covers about a centimetre in it. No flap big enough to cover a sensor could swing open and shut again that fast, so the camera doesn't try. Two curtains cross the sensor, one after the other, and the picture is made in the gap between them.

The numbers on this page come from one well-documented shutter, the one Nikon built into its FE2 film camera in 1983, whose curtains crossed the frame in . The mechanical shutter in a digital camera is the same machine, sitting in front of a sensor instead of film.

Where is the shutter?

Follow the light into the camera. The lens bends it so that every point of the scene comes back together as a point on one flat plane behind it, the focal plane. The sensor sits exactly there. The shutter sits a few millimetres in front of it, which is why it is called a focal-plane shutter.

The shutter itself is a thin sandwich. A metal frame has a window cut in it the size of the picture, 36 by 24 millimetres. In front of the window run two curtains, each made of a handful of thin blades. When the camera is ready to shoot, the first curtain is spread across the window like a closed blind, and the second curtain is folded away above it.

Below, the parts come apart along the path of the light. Drag the slider to hold them open, and point at a part to pick it out.

Look at the sensor. The lens turns the picture upside down, so the top of the sensor holds the bottom of the scene. That detail will matter twice further down.

How can a curtain make a thousandth of a second?

Press the button and the first curtain falls, uncovering the sensor from the top down. Some time later the second curtain falls after it and covers the sensor again, also from the top down. Both take the same to cross. The shutter speed you choose sets only one thing: how long the second curtain waits before it follows.

Below, the window on the left is the shutter seen from the lens. The chart on the right plots both curtains on one clock, and its rows line up with the window's rows. The dotted band between the two lines is the time each row spends uncovered, with light falling on it.

At 1/125 s the second curtain waits so long that the whole sensor sits uncovered for before it sets off. At 1/1000 s it leaves when the first curtain has crossed less than a third of the frame, and what crosses the sensor is no longer an open window but a slit tall. It is a narrow slit moving at a steady speed. Minolta's Maxxum 9xi of 1992 pushed the idea to 1/12,000 s, with a slit 1.1 millimetres wide.

Why does the flash have a speed limit?

A flash is far shorter than most of these exposures. Nikon's SB-910 flashgun lasts about at full power and as little as 1/38,500 s at its lowest. The camera fires it at one particular instant: when the first curtain finishes crossing. For the whole picture to catch the flash, the whole window has to be uncovered at that instant.

That only happens if the second curtain waits at least one full crossing, , before it sets off. The fastest standard speed that waits that long is 1/250 s, or 4 milliseconds, which leaves the frame fully open for . That is its flash sync speed. Below, the flash lasts , the SB-910 at a quarter of its power, and the room is otherwise dark.

Go one step faster and the second curtain is already part of the way down when the flash fires. The top rows of the sensor are covered and stay dark. The lens turned the picture upside down, so in the photo the dark band sits along the bottom. At 1/4000 s only a thin strip at the top of the photo catches any of the flash at all. This is why a camera with an ordinary flash switched on usually won't let you pick a faster speed. Some flashes get around it by flickering rapidly for the whole crossing, like a very bright lamp, at the cost of much of their power.

The direction the curtains travel matters too. Leica's M3 of 1954 ran cloth curtains sideways across the long 36 mm side, taking 18 milliseconds, so its whole frame was open only at about 1/50 s and slower. Crossing the short side, 24 mm, is part of how sync speeds reached 1/250 s.

What happens to something that moves?

Every row gets the same short exposure, but not at the same moment. Whatever moves while the slit crosses is caught in a slightly different place in each row.

The most famous example is a photograph Jacques Henri Lartigue took as a teenager at the French Grand Prix, in 1912 or 1913 (the sources disagree). The car's wheel came out as an oval leaning forward, and the spectators beside the road lean back, as if the car had blown them over. He had swung the camera to follow the car while the shutter's slit crossed the picture.

Below, the picture is shown the right way up, so the slit climbs it from the bottom. Rows below the slit are already recorded; above it the scene is still moving. The slider sets how fast the camera swings to follow the car.

With the camera still, only the car moves, so only the car leans. Follow it perfectly and the car stands up straight while the poles and the crowd lean back instead. A swing somewhere in between gives both, which is what Lartigue's picture shows.

In a modern camera the effect is much smaller. A car at 100 km/h moves about while a curtain crosses the frame, so a lean shows only on very fast subjects or behind slow shutters.

How do the blades get across so fast?

Each curtain has to cross 24 mm in . That is an average of , about , the pace of a fast cyclist, and then it has to stop at the end without bouncing back into the picture.

The trick is in how the blades are hung. In one Nikon design each curtain has four blades, and all four ride on the same two arms. The arms turn on pivots beside the window, and a spring wound round one pivot swings them. Each blade is pinned to both arms at the same distance from their pivots, so the two arms and the blade always make a parallelogram and the blade stays level as it moves. But each blade is pinned at a different distance. When the arms swing, the farthest blade sweeps through a long arc and the nearest barely moves.

One swing of the arms spreads four short blades into a wall taller than the window, or folds them into a stack below it. In the drawing, the far blade, the one whose edge forms the slit, moves nearly four times as far as the near one in the same moment, so it is the fastest-moving part of the shutter.

In many electronic shutters a magnet holds each cocked curtain against its spring: the camera lets go of the first, counts out the exposure, then lets go of the second. The blades are made as light as possible. Nikon's FM2 of 1982 used titanium foil etched with a honeycomb pattern; Minolta's 9xi used duralumin and carbon fibre.

Does a camera without curtains still have the slit?

Your phone has no curtains at all. Its sensor starts and stops each row's exposure electronically and reads the rows out one after another, just as the slit crossed them. The slit has moved into the chip. It is called a rolling shutter, and it can be much slower than a curtain.

A Sony a7 IV, an ordinary full-frame camera, takes about 1/15 s to read its whole sensor in silent mode. Nikon's Z9, whose sensor is built for speed, takes about 1/270 s, close to the FE2's curtains from 1983. In 2023 Sony's a9 III became the first full-frame camera with a global shutter, a sensor that exposes every row at once. It can fire a flash at any speed up to 1/80,000 s.

Below, one propeller turning at rpm, the speed of a small plane's engine near full throttle, is photographed through four kinds of shutter at the same instant.

The curtain bends the blades a little. The slow sensor breaks them into pieces that were never there: by the time it reaches the top row, the propeller has turned . Only the global shutter shows the propeller as it was.

So the next time a photo shows a bent propeller or a leaning car, it isn't a glitch. You are looking at the slit, and at a picture that was taken one row at a time.
