# Creative method: past the first idea

The user's note on @shapelayer's "What is creativity" (references.md): creativity should come from inside, not from the references. Left alone, the first ideas are the most likely ones: the genre's default shot, the reference's own move, the stock symbol (a light bulb for an idea, a clock for time, a rocket for growth). Run these steps before the shot list, and keep everything in `docs/concepts.md` so the critic can see what was killed and why.

## 0. Rewrite the brief first
On prompt-motion.com, 27 of the 41 type, shape and particle films came from one generic prompt ("a showreel, go all out"), and they came out as one film: the median. The ones that stood out had been given, or gave themselves, three things the generic brief lacks:
- **a subject with real facts** (a photon's 170,000-year walk out of the Sun with live depth and temperature; what happens on Earth in one second);
- **a ban on the defaults** (two creators banned "frames and texts on the corners" by name, and both films came out cleaner and more typographic);
- **a rule for how the words behave** (each key word given a physical job in the frame; the cue sheet written by phrase, with one held stillness).

So before step 1, write the brief you wish you'd been given, in four lines: the subject and its real numbers, what's banned, how the words behave, and the one feeling at the end.

## 1. Inventory the subject (25+ entries)
List real things from the subject's own world, not adjectives:
- materials and textures
- the sounds it makes, its motions and physics
- its tools and its units (a heart rate, a pace, an RPM, a file size)
- its rituals and its failures
- its smallest part and its biggest scale

For a brand, read its site, docs and changelog. For footage, list everything that's in the frame (the coat, the stair rail, the light) and everything the person does.

## 2. Write 20 concepts
Write each concept as one sentence, "the <technique> is literally <subject thing>" or "<subject> as <a different world>".

Force collisions: pair inventory entries with an unrelated domain (a tool for making things, a natural process, a machine, the body). @shapelayer's piece pairs a design tool's selection box and cursor with cells budding. Neither half alone would be creative.

## 3. Kill the first five
They're the most probable ones. Write them down so you know what they were, then strike them. Also strike:
- the AI-showreel defaults: corner brackets with a timecode and frame counter (about 20 of the 41), a rippling dot grid and a particle sphere with orbit rings (about 10 each), a dot that becomes every full stop (about 10, usually orange), a cubic-bezier curve editor (6), a metaball blob, a hexagon tunnel, skill bars, "AVAILABLE FOR HIRE";
- a word doing its dictionary meaning ("STRETCH" stretched, "BLUR" blurred): the showreel's first idea. A word's job has to come from the subject (step 5b);
- any concept a reference already did;
- anything an earlier film here did (`examples/`, `demos/`);
- any stock symbol.

If a concept could be described with the reference's own sentence (the same-sentence test in SKILL.md), it's gone.

## 4. Set two hard constraints
Pick two, for example:
- one colour plus black;
- no cuts;
- only the subject's own footage;
- every transition made of the subject's own material;
- the edit's clock is a number from the subject.

Constraints push you to invent. "Make it cool" doesn't.

## 5. Make one invention ("only this could exist here")
Every piece carries one device that would make no sense for any other subject.

**Test:** swap in a competitor or another person. If the device still works unchanged, it isn't the invention.

## 5b. Give the key words a job (type-led pieces)
Pick the 3–5 words that carry the line and give each one a role in the subject's world that the frame can show: a word that is a load the others hold up, a count that is the edit's clock, a number whose size is its distance. The best type film here built its last line on a platform made from the earlier words; another set each line in the voice of what it claimed (a wonky word pulled straight by a ruled line).

A persistent prop works the same way: one object from the subject that becomes a part of each word or the cut between shots (a bolt's afterimage that becomes the underline). A dot that becomes the full stop is not that prop any more: about 10 of the 41 used one.

## 6. Give every borrowed technique a meaning
Each technique from a reference must mean something in the subject's world. The pre-drop silence becomes a flatline; a speed ramp becomes a held breath; a block swap becomes a cell dividing. If a technique is there only because the reference had it, cut it.

## 7. Check for novelty against the sheets
- Tile your stills next to every reference's contact sheet. Any shot that reads as theirs scores 4 or less and gets redesigned.
- Then ask the fresh critic (SKILL.md step 5) one extra question: "which shot could you have predicted from the brief alone?" Redesign that one too.

## 8. Write the logline last
Write it after the invention exists: one sentence a stranger could repeat.

## Worked example (the aura-edit Pulse demo)
- **Subject:** 3.4 s of a medical student in a white coat on a stairwell.
- **Killed:**
  - slow-mo walk with a "DOCTOR" title;
  - a stethoscope heart pulsing on the beat;
  - an ECG line as decoration behind the type;
  - a study montage;
  - a "future Dr." caption with a glow;
  - flashcards strobing (#24's quote deck again).
- **Constraints:** only her 3.4 s of footage; the tempo comes from a pulse.
- **Invention:** the edit's clock is a heartbeat. It rests at 75 BPM, doubles to the drop's 150, and the measured pre-drop silence becomes a flatline (one held monitor tone, the frame frozen, the trace flat). The drop is the shock that restarts it, drawn as an X-ray negative impact frame.
- **Borrowed, with a meaning:**
  - #24's stills strobe becomes scan slices of one frame at rising zoom;
  - the BPM readout is the music's real tempo;
  - the light sweep follows the stair rail's diagonal.
